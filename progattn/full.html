Attention transplants carry routing, and synthesized programs carry none of it

Gene Yang · 31 August 2026 · Based on Explaining Attention with Program Synthesis1

We investigate whether initializing a fresh GPT-2-style transformer with programmatic attention patterns confers a convergence speedup, and if so, what predicts it. We find that it offers a slight speedup compared to baselines — no larger than the same programs shuffled onto the wrong documents — but is significantly worse than initializing with the exact attention patterns, at least under the training methods tried here. We then investigate three hypotheses to further understand the mechanism: whether any fixed prior does as much, if it's the candidate selection, or the loss of expressivity from freezing the QK patterns. All three fail, and one intervention locates the difference: re-pair real attention's patterns with other documents and the speedup collapses; every program variant is indifferent to the same operation, even though the programs also compute their patterns from the text. This gives the paper's “pruning effect” caveat exact form: a program preserves the model it is spliced into because the routing it replaces is rarely needed, not because it reproduces that routing — and a direct test makes it causal: replacing a head with its program costs the trained model, on average, what deleting the head costs. A dose-response closes the loop: interpolating each head's pattern from its document-mean template to its exact attention restores the speedup monotonically, from the programs' level at zero routing to exact's at full dose.

heads constrained

We measure each method by strict speedup: the baseline's steps to reach four validation-loss thresholds divided by this run's steps, geometric mean, defined only when every threshold is reached. 

We find that real attention provides a substantial speedup which disappears when patterns are shuffled, while programs offer a modest speedup invariant to head-pattern pairing. 

Motivation

Injecting attention patterns from a trained model into a fresh one is known to accelerate convergence2, and Hayes et al.1 synthesize Python programs that “reproduce those attention maps given only input text.” A trained head's weights cannot be transplanted — W_Q, W_K only mean something in the pretrained residual-stream basis — but a program is a function from token text to an attention map, independent of any model's internals. If the programs capture what the heads do, their patterns should confer the speedup the real patterns do.

The outcomes were charted before the first run. If exact attention helps and programs do not, the approximation dropped something that matters for learning. If both help alike, the symbolic structure carries the useful bias, and it is portable by construction. If neither helps, mature routing is a consequence of learned representations, not a scaffold for acquiring them. And if programs beat exact, the fit is denoising the learned circuit into a better prior — the most interesting case. The first is what happened: a large speedup for exact attention, a modest one for programs. So the question became what predicts the speedup a head — real or programmatic — confers.

For real heads there are predictors: the benefit rises with depth (ρ = +0.78 in the pilot) and with input-dependence — how much the head's attention moves with the text (ρ = +0.254 over all 144 heads, p = 0.002). For programs every predictor failed. IoU, the paper's own alignment metric, predicted nothing (IoU × importance ρ = −0.07, p = 0.42), and sparsity, which did predict program speedup on the 43 sink-free heads (ρ = −0.52, p = 4×10−4), turned out to price any sparse pattern rather than these programs: a pre-registered control giving each head a different program matched on row entropy did exactly as well. The programs' benefit was predictable only from generic properties of the pattern, never from anything tying a program to its head — the first sign that the pairing itself does nothing. What needed explaining was the gap, and the explanations we could think of all failed:

Part 1 · The result

Setup

It's known that injecting attention patterns learned from a trained model accelerates convergence2 : patch an attention head's post-emergence attention into a pre-emergence model and recover the capability, and report Gopalani and Hu's finding that learning the ground-truth attention map is the bottleneck behind abrupt learning3. Hayes et al.1 separately show that inside an already-trained model, a quarter of the heads can be replaced by their synthesized programs without substantial damage. Together these predict that a fresh transformer initialized with programmatic patterns should converge faster. This report tests that prediction.

How it is injected

Every headline number uses fixed: the injected head's QK circuit receives no gradient and cannot route on content. Under bias the head keeps that circuit and learns routing on top of the prior, whose strength is a learned per-head scale λ that starts at 1 and is free to fall to 0. It turns out the distinction does not matter much (Hypothesis 3). 

What the analysis finds

Three pattern sources are injected head by head — the head's real attention, the best-fit program assigned to it, and the program originally synthesized from it — each aligned (every document trains under the pattern computed from it) or shuffled (the same patterns re-paired with other documents under one fixed permutation). The shuffle keeps every pattern and every shape statistic and destroys only the correspondence to the text, so it separates what a pattern contains from what its pairing contributes. The two shuffled program conditions are different programs' patterns and measure identically (+1.36% against +1.35%, p = 0.35).

In training, a real head loses four fifths of its benefit when re-paired; both program variants lose nothing, despite the own-synthesized one carrying 3.3× the input-dependence. The programs also match the heads' sparsity (mean row entropy 1.76 against 1.81), so no property of the patterns themselves explains the gap; the benefit rides on the pairing. What the programs do transfer is real but small — +0.34% over all 144 heads, above both baseline and an empty causal matrix (p ≤ 2×10−4) — and indifferent to which document it is paired with.

The 144-head sweep ran one condition per head at one seed. Mismatching needs four conditions and three seeds, so it runs on 16 heads: the eight with the largest benefit from real attention above a 0.5 attention-sink threshold, and the eight largest below 0.3.

Part 2 · Three explanations, falsified
Hypothesis 1

Any stable attention prior helps early training

Could the programs' small gain be generic — any stable prior beating none, a regularizer rather than content? The pilot column reads that way: every structural baseline helps there, causal-uniform above the best fit itself. If so, what a program computes is irrelevant and the conditions table's ordering is the whole story.

Falsified. An empty causal prior is worth +0.01% over 144 heads, and under bias it is mathematically a no-op.
Evidence

The paper's non-program baselines put structure without content into the same harness. Causal-uniform and random causal logits reach +0.83% and +0.71% on the 12 pilot heads, but over all 144 heads causal-uniform collapses to +0.01%, with 51% of heads above zero — a coin flip. The pilot column reads the other way — uniform +0.83% above the best fit's +0.56% — but none of those pairwise differences is significant at n = 12, and the pilot's heads were selected for injection response, which inflates every prior; the unselected population and the K-sweep (program 1.02× against uniform 0.99× at K=16) both put programs above uniform. Under bias the calibration is exact: the log of causal-uniform is constant along each causal row, softmax ignores a per-row constant, so the injection is a no-op — its learned scale never moves off λ = 1.000 — and the +0.51% it posts on the 16-head set is the noise floor of a selected head set. A prior as such buys nothing; the programs' +0.34% must come from what they specifically contain, which the next hypothesis takes apart.

Hypothesis 2

The fit discards the input-dependence that carries the benefit

Could the library contain the routing while the fit throws it away? The selection step is document-averaged IoU, whose optimum is the mean-field pattern; the winners are far flatter than the library they come from; and input-dependence is the property prior work ties to faster convergence. If the discarding is the failure, restoring the dependence should restore the transfer.

Half confirmed, then falsified where it counts: the fit does select flat programs — none of the 46 head-level-dependent programs wins a head — but restoring the dependence recovers nothing.
Evidence

The 35 programs that win a head average 0.049, against a library mean of 0.161: 8.9 SD below a random draw from the same library, Mann-Whitney on the distinct winners p = 1.8×10−8. Two winning programs, holding 16 heads between them, emit an identical matrix for every document.

Scoring every program against every head recovers the same relation across all 20,736 pairs. IoU falls as input-dependence rises for 97 of 144 heads, median ρ = −0.334, Wilcoxon p = 1.4×10−11. This matrix is computed during fitting and then discarded, so the relation is available at no extra cost.

Input-dependence is the property that the convergence literature singles out. Puri et al.4 report that input-dependent sparse attention converges 1.37–8.83× faster than dense attention, while input-agnostic sparse attention gives no such benefit and often converges more slowly. A fit that selects against it is selecting against the mechanism.

Across the 144 heads the whole chain can be read off directly, and the mechanism is real where it should be: a head's own input-dependence predicts how much its exact attention helps (ρ = +0.254, p = 0.002). The pre-registered cousin of this test — dependence predicting mismatch damage over the 16-head set — failed on that set's narrow range and resolves with more heads, not more seeds. For programs the chain goes quiet: the winner's input-dependence only weakly tracks its transfer (ρ = +0.140, p = 0.09) and its IoU only weakly anti-tracks it (ρ = −0.155, p = 0.06). The intervention below says why: raising a program's input-dependence 3.3× moves transfer by +0.08 points, so these faint correlations are shadows of the fit, not a lever.

IoU is averaged over documents. A constant matrix placed at the mean attention pattern scores moderately on every document, while a program that is accurate on some documents and wrong on others is charged for the misses. The averaged objective has its optimum at the mean-field answer, which is the one with no routing in it.

The strictest version of the proposal: skip the re-ranking entirely and give every head the program synthesized from its own attention — provenance instead of a floor, at the library's mean input-dependence of 0.161. Across all 144 heads it delivers +0.42% against the best fit's +0.34% (p = 0.41), and on the 16-head set it is indifferent to mismatching (+0.03 points, p = 0.28). A floored-IoU fit at 0.240 remains untested, but the mechanism it was meant to restore has failed its direct test.

Hypothesis 3

The fixed rule freezes the circuits programs would need

Could the failure be the injection rule rather than the patterns? Under fixed the injected head's QK circuit receives no gradient and cannot learn any content routing — the harshest possible reading of “use this pattern.” A soft prior that leaves the circuit learning would rescue the programs if expressivity is what they lack.

Falsified. Rerun as a soft prior with every QK circuit learning, the ordering reproduces condition for condition, and heads amplify their program priors rather than escaping them.
Evidence

If programs fail only because fixed freezes the circuits, bias should rescue them. Under bias, on the same 16 heads and seeds:

The soft prior buys programs a single just-detectable pairing effect, +0.21 points aligned over shuffled (median, p = 0.010), thirty-fold smaller than the oracle's. At all 144 heads the picture holds (strict scoring, one seed): exact +3.90%, own program +0.67%, best-fit program +0.30%, causal-uniform +0.05% — the exact-over-program gap reproduces at +3.6 points (p = 2×10−16). The one soft-prior nuance: own programs edge best-fit by +0.36 points (p = 0.02 uncorrected), the same faint pairing signal the 16-head shuffle showed — two orders of magnitude below the oracle's.

Part 3 · What the programs actually do

The pruning effect, causal

If programs carry no routing, why does splicing one into a trained model preserve it at all?

At inference the dissociation is the same. Replacing one head inside the pretrained GPT-2 on 256 held-out documents: shuffled exact attention costs +0.0040 nats (p = 2×10−4 over 16 heads) where aligned exact costs nothing, and the best-fit program costs the same aligned as shuffled (+0.0046 against +0.0045, p = 0.73). The paper anticipated this reading: the pruning literature it cites finds many attention heads can be removed outright, and its limitations flag that the question-answering gains it sees at low replacement levels “may be akin to a pruning effect” — replacement helping because the head was dispensable, not because the program is faithful. The shuffle turns that caveat into a measurement. A program whose document correspondence has been destroyed cannot be a faithful proxy, yet it preserves the trained model exactly as well as the aligned program and speeds up a fresh model exactly as much — so all of the preservation, and all of the training benefit, is pruning-grade structure, none of it captured routing. The pruning comparison makes this causal: on the same heads and documents, program replacement costs +0.0046 nats, mean-ablating the head — the pruning literature's operation — costs +0.0047 (paired p = 1.0), and the head's own document-mean pattern costs +0.0029; only a generic uniform pattern does worse (+0.0124, p < 0.001). A program preserves the trained model no better than deleting the head, and what it does preserve, the head's positional template carries. This sits against the framing that programs “reproduce those attention maps given only input text”1.

The mean hides both signs. Per head, the paired difference between program and deletion runs from −0.019 nats (L4H11, the program far cheaper than deleting the head) to +0.017 (L11H8, far costlier): some programs preserve function beyond pruning and others actively hurt, and what separates them is open. The equality is a statement about the average, not about any head.

How much routing buys the speedup back

The head's own routing restores the speedup in proportion to the dose, from the programs' level at zero routing to exact's at full strength, monotonically.
Evidence

The ladder's gentlest replacement was the head's own document-mean pattern — its shape with the routing removed — and that template is one end of a dial. The template alone gives +1.51%, indistinguishable from the best-fit program's +1.35% (p = 0.22) — the program floor reached a second way, with the head's own shape in place of a symbolic fit. Every dose of routing is significant (+2.25 points over program already at λ = ¼, p = 4×10−5), and the curve is concave: the first quarter of the routing restores 47% of the program-to-exact gap, half restores 72%, three quarters 91%, and the last quarter is still real (exact over λ = ¾ by +0.45 points, p = 3×10−4). What a dependence floor on the fit could not supply, the head's own variation supplies dose for dose: what matters is not how much a pattern moves with the text but whether it moves the way the head does.

A prediction that reversed

The pre-registration predicted that attention-sink heads, holding two thirds of their mass at position 0, would be close to document-invariant and so lose little to mismatching. Sink heads score 0.289 on input-dependence against 0.272 for clean heads. A sink is a large constant offset together with highly variable routing in its remaining third, and that residual carries the largest benefit in the set: sink mass predicts the routing component at ρ = +0.821, p = 0.0001.

All 73 heads above the sink threshold are fitted with a first_column program, which reproduces the offset exactly and drops the residual.

Method, and what these numbers do not cover

A 12-layer, 12-head, 384-dim model trained on TinyStories at 128 tokens, batch 48, 4000 steps, with the output head remapped to the tokens the training corpus uses. 1,508 training runs across the six sweeps reported here, besides the twelve-head pilot that fills the first column of the conditions table. Cost is final validation loss paired within seed; seed SD is 0.0024 nats. Input-dependence is measured over 256 held-out documents.

Strict speedup censors: a run that misses a threshold contributes no speedup, which drops the slowest runs and so flatters the condition that fails most. The censoring is small and runs against the headline gap. Every 16-head design passes all four thresholds in every run; in the two 144-head sweeps 97.9–100% of runs pass per condition — the failures are almost all layer-0 heads, each reaching three of four — and imputing every failed run at its condition's worst observed speedup moves no mean by more than 0.10 points and flips no contrast. At K=144 no run passes any threshold and speedup is reported as undefined rather than on a surviving subset; final loss, which every run defines, carries the cost claims there.

The 16 heads were selected on an outcome from a prior sweep, which inflates both arms of a paired contrast equally and cancels in the difference, but means nothing here generalizes to heads that real attention does not help. Three seeds for the mismatch experiment in both injection modes, four for the cost curve, three for the dose-response, one for each 144-head sweep. The inference tests use the screening harness — interchange interventions on the pretrained GPT-2 over the 256 screening documents, batch 16 — and report change in mean next-token cross-entropy.

Scope. Everything here is one corpus (TinyStories, the paper's own choice), one fresh model (12×12×384), patterns from GPT-2-small, a 4,000-step budget; the inference tests measure cross-entropy, not the paper's question-answering accuracy. Two sub-noise-order content signals under bias remain unexplained: aligned programs over shuffled by +0.21 points (p = 0.010) and own programs over best-fit by +0.36 points (p = 0.02, uncorrected). And nothing here tests whether a symbolic rule outside this library — one that genuinely tracks the text, an induction-style match-previous-token pattern, say — could carry routing through the same interface; the library's programs cannot, but that is a fact about the library and its fit, not yet about symbolic routing as such.

Hypotheses, thresholds and head sets were fixed in a pre-registration written before either training job ran. Of its seven testable predictions, one held: that mismatching real attention costs speedup. Two held only at K=144 rather than across K as stated, and four failed outright, including the sink prediction above and the prediction that input-dependence would predict mismatch damage across the 16 heads (ρ = +0.20, p = 0.46, on a range of only 0.21–0.45); the corresponding relation over all 144 heads is significant (Hypothesis 3). Hypotheses 2 and 3 and their sub-analyses were formulated after seeing the mismatch result and were not pre-registered.
